Essay · Introduction to “AI” · September 2026

The Can Opener and the CanAn introduction to “AI” for the curious

A Fifty-Year-Old in a Romper Suit

Technology, as everyone knows, lags behind its ideas, and today's “AI” is the finest example: a fifty-year-old in a newborn's romper suit. The ingredients are old. Artificial “neurons” date from 1943, the perceptron from 1958. The method by which networks can be set up from examples, the backward distribution of error (“backpropagation”), was described in the mid-1970s. One can picture it as a formal complaint procedure: the network guesses, gets it wrong, and the error is passed backwards layer by layer until every department acknowledges an appropriate share of the blame and changes its settings. These computing nodes are about as closely related to the neurons in your head as a paper plane is to a lark: both fly, but only one of them lays eggs.

What is new is above all the quantity (data, computing power) and the transformer architecture of 2017. That multilayer networks came into wide use only in the mid-1980s had several causes: computing power, money, and the competition from symbolic AI. Added to that was an oversimplification of the story that I call “narrative collapse”. In 1969 Minsky and Papert cleanly proved what single-layer networks cannot do, and considered more layers probably unpromising, since no workable learning procedure was in sight. Together with the successes of symbolic AI, this turned for a time into pessimism about networks of any kind. It is as if someone had shown that a chair with one leg tips over, and then declared furniture with more legs not worth the effort because nobody knew how to build it. I myself used multilayer networks back then for production control and for consumer classification in the market research that Bielefeld had in abundance.

Structured Echolalia and What It Leaves Out

Treating language as a pattern was, for us practitioners, rather a running gag with which one could tease humanities scholars among one's acquaintances. In research it was attempted quite seriously, for instance by Jeffrey Elman from 1990. The post-structuralist autonomy of language espoused by those acquaintances (“The Death of the Author”) was, after all, suspiciously similar to the fantasies of today's AI financiers. Laurent Binet condensed this into a crime novel, The Seventh Function of Language. There, Roland Barthes is not simply run over by a laundry van in 1980 but murdered because he knew of a forbidden seventh function of language:

“Let us imagine a function of language that could, far more extensively, convince anyone of anything in any situation. […] Whoever knew and mastered this function would be, practically, the master of the world. His power would be boundless. He could get himself elected in any election, mobilise the masses, trigger revolutions, seduce women, sell any conceivable product, build empires, deceive the whole world, get everything he wants. … In fact, everything suggests that semiotics is one of the central inventions in the history of humankind, one of the sharpest instruments man has ever forged. But, as with fire and nuclear fission, at first one does not know what it is good for or how to use it.”

Laurent Binet, La septième fonction du langage (German edition: “Die siebte Sprachfunktion”); quoted here in the author's own English translation from the German

Billions are flowing today into the wager that someone has found exactly this function. The only question is whether it was a network that found it, or a very diligent parrot.

I once called the method “structured echolalia”. For pretraining the nickname fits: a language model first reads vast quantities of text, including an enormous slice of the internet, books and program code, and learns to continue any text plausibly. As a complete description it is too short. First, upbringing follows: many of today's models go through further stages of training in which people rate answers and desirable behaviour is reinforced; on tasks with automatically checkable results (arithmetic, programming) the model can additionally learn from success and failure. The reader who has devoured half the library becomes an employee with manners. Second, the inner life is richer than mere parroting. Interpretability researchers at Anthropic found evidence in Claude 3.5 Haiku that the model picks the rhyme word before the line is written (though the method makes only part of the computation visible). A parrot that chooses the rhyme word before the sentence is no poet, but no longer a parrot either. Whether one should call that “understanding” is a fine question for the evening. For the working day it hardly matters.

Three Peculiarities You Don't Know from Software

Reliability is measured, not derived. For small networks and narrowly defined properties there are proofs and verification methods. For a large general-purpose model in an open environment there is no complete behavioural guarantee, and the reason is fundamental: neither the possible range of inputs nor the desired behaviour can be described conclusively, and what cannot be specified cannot be proven. It is like a baker whose recipe book nobody can read. One tastes loaves, takes samples, and hopes the customers order rolls again tomorrow. (This is not entirely foreign to ordinary software: it, too, is mostly tested rather than proven. Only its parts can be secured individually; a network has no parts in that sense.)

You can't turn a screw in just one place. Networks can be influenced: through data, further training and instructions. But unlike a program, where you change one condition and know what will happen, things here are more like sourdough: you change flour, warmth and time, but not one particular bubble in the dough.

Simple building blocks surprise their inventors. In 1955 Fermi, Pasta, Ulam and the programmer Mary Tsingou let a chain of masses and slightly nonlinear springs run on one of the first mainframe computers. The expectation was that the energy would distribute itself obediently over all modes of vibration. Instead the system almost returned to its starting state, as if it were homesick. These were not neural networks, but it was a lasting lesson: computers let loose on simple nonlinear systems contradict intuition with astonishing regularity. With networks this continues. Some first memorise their task and, long afterwards, suddenly begin to generalise (“grokking”). And when a physics journal in 2025 draws on a spring–block model, familiar from earthquake physics, to explain learning in deep networks (Shi, Pan, Dokmanić, Physical Review Letters), that says something about the distance between knowing how and understanding. That is no reason to belittle the progress of recent decades.

What Makes the Whole Thing Cheap

The flip side of these peculiarities is four conveniences. Large models can be boiled down like stock: through quantisation, pruning and distillation, much smaller models can emerge, and with specialised training they become specialists for narrower tasks. That is not free and not lossless, but it works. Many models are available as open weights for download and run on suitably powerful local machines, where a few years ago a data centre seemed necessary. Data protection thereby becomes a question of architecture. The most robust approach is never to let the data leave the house at all. With rented models, sealed-off compute enclaves in the hardware (so-called Trusted Execution Environments) help, whereas “computing on encrypted data” is, for language models, still more research than everyday practice. And finally, people talk to these systems as to people. Weizenbaum's ELIZA managed that in 1966 with a few rules. Fluent language is spontaneously taken for expertise, and that is the greatest gain in convenience and the greatest danger in one.

The Can Opener and the Can

AI is a wonderfully useful tool, just as the can opener is one. But the can opener is an invention with a delay: the tin can came around 1810, the opener roughly half a century later. Whoever wanted beans until then took hammer and chisel. With AI it is partly the other way round: the opener is here, but many of the fitting cans still have to be constructed. And cans are no small matter. They need form, material, standardisation, and the trust that there really are beans inside.

Translated: a particularly good “can” is a task whose result can be checked far more cheaply than produced. Examples are proofs that a proof checker waves through, programs against which tests run, and answers whose claims can be traced one by one to passages in their sources. Then the language model may guess, as long as behind it sits an accountant who does not guess. Whoever instead uses the opener as a screwdriver, for instance as an unchecked or solely responsible authority for diagnoses, legal advice or the supervision of a power plant, will find it a strangely unreliable tool and injure their fingers in the process.

Meanwhile a considerable part of the public debate argues about whether the can opener has a soul and whether it will one day open us. Whoever is hungry asks more practically: where are the cans?
